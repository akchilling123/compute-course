Day 13 · Week 2 · workloads

Quantization changes the replica

Compression is not a free speed button. It is a way to move a model across a hardware boundary, then test whether lower precision pays after quality, cache, and serving overhead.

From bits to the bill

Yesterday's 70B model needs roughly 140 GB of BF16 weight memory. At idealized FP8, its weights shrink to about 70 GB; at idealized 4-bit weight precision, 35 GB. These are weight-only lower bounds. Scales, metadata, activations, and KV cache consume additional memory, and the storage format may not match the arithmetic exactly. NVIDIA's TensorRT-LLM guide says lower precision can improve throughput and latency, but output quality must be checked.

The gain is discrete, the tradeoff is continuous

Take an illustrative $2/H100-hour rental rate. If a working BF16 replica needs two H100s, its GPU floor is $4/hour. If a quantized implementation fits with the required context and concurrency on one H100, its GPU floor is $2/hour: $48/day rather than $96/day for 24-hour operation. This is a conditional 50% hardware-floor cut, not a promised production saving. Throughput, quality, serving software, and actual utilization decide cost per accepted token. FP8's 70 GB of weights may still leave too little cache to satisfy the workload; four-bit compression buys more headroom but is not automatically faster on every kernel.

There is measured upside, but not a universal multiplier. In one NVIDIA TensorRT-LLM Llama 3.3 70B case study distributed over four H100 SXM 80GB GPUs, tuned FP16 throughput was 2,474 tokens/s and tuned FP8 throughput 6,049 tokens/s, about 2.44×. Its FP8 KV cache change separately raised a reported baseline from 3,390 to 5,300 tokens/s, about 1.56×. Different tuned configurations, workloads, and quality gates can give different answers. That benchmark is evidence that quantization can materially change realized economics, not proof that a 70B model will meet this lesson's illustrative single-H100 case.

Do not compress only the weights on a spreadsheet. NVIDIA's guide notes that its KV cache is not quantized by default; enabling FP8 KV-cache quantization may improve throughput but raises quality risk. Memory freed in weights can be spent on longer contexts or more parallel requests, which may be more valuable than removing a GPU.

Rules of thumb

1 · Model the step function.Ask which precision crosses a feasible GPU-per-replica boundary with enough cache left over.
2 · Gate on answer quality.A cheaper token that fails your evaluation or needs a retry is not a cheaper useful answer.
3 · Measure cost per accepted token.Use the real workload's context, batch, quality pass rate, and achieved throughput, not the format's theoretical compression ratio.

Questions for the CFO table

  • Which precision gives us the best measured cost per accepted output on our actual prompts?
  • Does the compressed model really fit one GPU at target concurrency, or did the KV cache consume the savings?
  • Could the same freed HBM produce more revenue as longer context or higher batch capacity?

Day 12 · Week 2 · workloads

Model size is a fleet decision

Parameter count becomes a memory bill before it becomes an intelligence claim. If the weights do not fit, the serving plan changes: more GPUs per replica, less room for concurrent requests, or a smaller representation.

Translate parameters into hardware

A simple first pass: weight memory = parameters × bytes per parameter. At BF16, each parameter takes 2 bytes. Meta's Llama 3 family spans 8B, 70B, and 405B parameters. Their weights alone would occupy about 16, 140, and 810 decimal GB. This is a lower bound, not a deployment requirement: KV cache, activations, runtime buffers, and fragmentation need extra HBM.

Bar lengths scale to the 405B footprint; the H100 multiples divide weight bytes by nominal 80 GB. They are not a practical GPU count. The integer weight-only floors are 1, 2, and 11 H100s; real serving needs headroom and compatible sharding.

A 70B BF16 model cannot fit on one 80 GB H100; it crosses the two-GPU floor before serving a request. It nominally fits in a 141 GB H200 with only 1 GB left for everything else, so that is not a viable one-GPU serving design as stated. This is the trap in sizing a fleet from parameter count alone. The 405B model needs at least 11 H100s or 6 H200s just for weights, before any serving headroom, and practical tensor parallelism may require a different count.

Memory speed becomes output speed

During single-stream autoregressive decode, weights are repeatedly accessed to generate tokens. A rough optimistic roofline is memory bandwidth divided by model weight bytes, not a throughput guarantee. On an H100 SXM with 3.35 TB/s and an 8B BF16 model with 16 GB of weights: 3,350 ÷ 16 ≈ 209 tokens/second. On an H200 SXM, 4,800 ÷ 140 ≈ 34 tokens/second for 70B BF16 if it could fit with headroom. It cannot as a full serving stack on nominal 141 GB, so this second figure isolates bandwidth only, not a deployable configuration. Actual speed is lower and depends on implementation, context, batching, and interconnect.

Worked fleet floor

Illustrative GPU rental assumption: $2 per GPU-hour. At 70B BF16, two H100s start at $4 per replica-hour, or $96 per continuous day, before networking, CPU, energy, reserved slack, and cache. A single H200 is not the $2 alternative for an uncompressed 70B model without room for cache. The finance question is not "how big is the model?" but "what is the cheapest working replica with our context and concurrency?" Tomorrow's quantization lesson changes that threshold.

1 · Fit weights plus working memory.Nominal capacity is not usable serving capacity. Reserve space for context and concurrency before declaring a one-GPU design.
2 · Count whole replicas.A model that crosses an HBM boundary can double the minimum GPUs per serving replica. Price the discrete step, not only gigabytes.
3 · Separate stream and fleet rates.Bandwidth caps single-stream decode; batching shares the same reads. Measure achieved aggregate tokens per dollar for fleet plans.

  • At our actual batch and stream mix, are we buying HBM capacity, HBM bandwidth, or compute?
  • How much of the budget disappears when a model upgrade crosses a GPU-per-replica boundary?

Day 11 · Week 2 · workloads

Latency as a paid feature

Day 10 bought throughput with waiting. The market sells the reverse: the same tokens, faster, at a premium. A latency SLA is a cost commitment with a price tag on both sides.

One model, three prices, set by the clock

Anthropic's price list already sells time. Opus 5 costs $5 / $25 per million input / output tokens at standard speed. Its fast mode, a research preview promising significantly faster output, costs exactly double: $10 / $50. Its Batch API, which processes asynchronously, costs exactly half. Same model, same tokens: a 4× price band set purely by when the answer arrives.

Anthropic list prices: fast mode is a research preview on Opus 5 and 4.8; Batch API pricing is 50% off standard. The 2× and 0.5× multipliers are the market's current read on what speed and delay are worth.

Customers pay for speed because latency converts to money on their side too. A Google and Deloitte study of mobile commerce found a 0.1-second speed improvement lifted retail conversions 8.4% and travel conversions 10.1%. Interactive AI products sit on the same curve: time-to-first-token decides whether a chat product feels alive, and stream rate decides whether a coding assistant keeps a developer in flow.

Why speed is expensive to sell

Speed is unbatched capacity. Day 10 showed the cost of a single stream can run 50× a full batch; a fast lane is a promise to keep batches small and accelerators reserved, which means sacrificing the throughput that made serving cheap. The provider's math: illustrative cost of $1.14 per million output tokens in a standard mixed regime becomes roughly $3–4 in a small-batch fast lane. The price doubles while cost triples off a small base, so the speed premium is mostly margin for whoever owns the scheduler. The batch discount runs the other way: half price for requests that fill idle off-peak capacity, turning Day 1's utilization problem into a product.

Time-to-first-token and stream rate are different products. TTFT is set by prefill and queue depth; tokens-per-second is set by decode, which is memory-bandwidth bound (Day 12). A latency SLA should say which one it buys, because they cost different things.

Rules of thumb

1 · Price speed from sacrificed throughput.A fast tier costs what the lost batching cost. Never set a latency SLA before you know its fleet footprint.
2 · Sell waiting.A 50% async discount costs little if it fills hours that would idle anyway. Discounting delay is utilization management wearing a price tag.
3 · Route by latency need, not by model habit.Chat needs TTFT; nightly jobs need nothing. Sending delay-tolerant traffic through the fast lane is pure cost with no revenue attached.

Questions for the CFO table

  • Which of our traffic actually pays a latency premium, and what multiple of standard price does it carry?
  • What would our latency SLA cost in sacrificed throughput and reserved fleet if every customer exercised it at once?
  • What share of volume could move to async batch at half price, and what would that do to off-peak utilization?
Sources and assumptions. Opus 5 standard ($5/$25 per million input/output tokens), fast mode ($10/$50, research preview on Opus 5 and 4.8), Batch API 50% discount, and cache reads at 0.1× input price: Anthropic API pricing. Latency-to-conversion evidence (0.1s mobile speed improvement: retail conversions +8.4%, travel +10.1%): Deloitte and Google, Milliseconds Make Millions (2020). Day 10 · Week 2 · workloads

Batching and throughput

An unbatched GPU is a truck hauling one box at a time. Batching multiplies the tokens each GPU-hour produces, and unit cost is just the hourly price divided by that number.

The biggest lever in serving cost

Day 9 gave the formula: cost per million tokens = $/GPU-hour ÷ achieved tokens per hour. Batching is how you move the denominator. A GPU serving one request spends most of each second waiting on memory; serving many requests in the same pass keeps it fed. The serving stack learned this twice over. Orca introduced iteration-level scheduling in 2022, batching at every generation step instead of every request, and showed a 36.9× throughput gain over NVIDIA FasterTransformer on GPT-3 175B at the same latency. vLLM's PagedAttention then attacked the memory side, cutting KV-cache waste to under 4% and delivering up to 24× the throughput of HuggingFace Transformers.

36.9× Orca throughput gain at equal latency (OSDI 2022, GPT-3 175B)
up to 24× vLLM vs. HuggingFace Transformers throughput
<4% KV-cache memory waste with PagedAttention
Memory sets the batch, the batch sets the cost. How many requests can share one GPU is capped by KV-cache space in HBM (Day 5), not by compute. That is why the serving stack spends its cleverness on memory management: every sequence squeezed in is throughput gained at zero extra hardware cost.

Worked example: one GPU, three regimes

Illustrative inputs: a 70B model on one H100 at $2.00 per hour. Serve it three ways: a single interactive stream at 30 tokens per second, a modest batch at 300, and a full continuous batch at 1,500. Same GPU, same model, same hourly price. Only the achieved throughput changes.

A 50× cost swing with zero change in hardware, model, or price list. This is why two providers can sell the same open model at very different prices and both be rational: they are running different batch regimes, and the cheaper one has the traffic to keep batches full. Throughput economics favor the busy, a serving-side echo of Day 1's utilization lesson.

Rules of thumb

1 · Price the achieved, not the peak.Serving unit cost is hourly price divided by achieved tokens per hour. Benchmarks give peaks; production gives the number that belongs in the model.
2 · Batch what can wait.Evals, extraction, summaries, and overnight jobs belong in throughput mode. Every request that tolerates delay is cheaper to serve; Day 11 prices the ones that cannot.
3 · Full batches need full order books.Batching gains only exist with enough concurrent demand. A quiet cluster in throughput mode is just an idle cluster: utilization first, batching second.

Questions for the CFO table

  • What achieved tokens per second per GPU do we get in production, by model, and how far is that from the assumption in our unit-cost model?
  • What share of our traffic is delay-tolerant and could shift into high-throughput serving or off-peak batch windows?
  • When we quote $/million tokens internally, which batching regime produced the number, and would it survive losing our largest customer's traffic?

Day 09 · Week 2 · workloads

Tokens, FLOPs, and finance translation

Product speaks tokens, infrastructure speaks GPU-hours, finance speaks dollars. The conversion is three multiplications and one leak called MFU.

The translation chain

Every AI P&L is the same conversion done twice, once for training and once for serving. Tokens of demand become FLOPs of compute (about 2 × parameters per token, from Day 8), FLOPs become GPU-seconds (through the chip's peak rate, discounted by efficiency), and GPU-seconds become dollars (at the $/GPU-hour from Day 2). Get the chain right and a product forecast drops straight into a capacity plan and a cost line.

2N FLOPs per token: 2 × parameter count (dense models, Day 8)
990 dense BF16 TFLOPS peak per H100
38–43% MFU Meta achieved training Llama 3 405B: the leak between peak and delivered
Never price off datasheet peak. Model FLOPs utilization is the share of peak compute a real workload actually extracts. Meta's flagship training run converted only 38–43% of peak; the rest went to communication, memory stalls, and restarts. A cost model built on peak specs understates cost by more than 2×.

Worked example: the serving side

Product asks: what does a billion tokens a day of 405B-class serving cost? Work the chain with illustrative inputs. One token costs 2 × 405 billion = 8.1 × 1011 FLOPs, so a billion tokens a day is 8.1 × 1020 FLOPs daily. An H100 at 40% MFU delivers about 1.43 × 1018 FLOPs per hour, so the workload needs about 568 GPU-hours a day, or 24 H100s running around the clock. At $2.00 per GPU-hour that is roughly $1,140 a day, or $1.14 per million tokens.

Sanity-check the floor against the market: Anthropic sells Haiku 4.5 at $1 per million input tokens and Sonnet 5 at $2. The floor and the price list live in the same neighborhood, which is the point: frontier-class serving costs measure in single dollars per million tokens, and small models in cents.

Worked example: the training side

The same chain runs backward from a training budget. Llama 3 405B used 3.8 × 1025 FLOPs. At 40% MFU on 990-TFLOPS H100s that is about 26 million H100-hours; at $2.00 per GPU-hour, roughly $53 million of compute, with Meta's disclosed 38–43% MFU putting the range near $50 to $56 million. That is the conversion Day 8's amortization example needs before training cost can be spread over served tokens: budget FLOPs, translate to GPU-hours, then to dollars, then to $ per million tokens at the volume you forecast.

Rules of thumb

1 · Speak one unit.Convert GPU-hours, FLOPs, and list prices into $ per million tokens before comparing anything. It is the only unit product, infrastructure, and finance all recognize.
2 · Apply MFU before money.Peak specs overstate delivered compute by 2× or more. Anchor on about 40% for frontier training, then stack Day 1 utilization on top.
3 · Parameters are the cost dial.Per-token cost scales linearly with model size. Halving the model halves the floor; routing small queries to small models is a margin decision, not an engineering nicety.

Questions for the CFO table

  • Who owns the tokens-to-GPU-hours conversion in our planning model, what MFU does it assume, and when was that assumption validated against the fleet?
  • What is our actual served cost per million tokens by model, and how does it compare to the prices we charge per million?
  • If product volume grows 10×, does our cost per million tokens fall, hold, or rise, and which term in the chain moves it?

Day 08 · Week 2 begins: workloads

Training vs. inference economics

Training is a factory you pay for once. Inference is a meter that never stops running. Which one dominates is a volume question, and volume is a forecast.

Two cost shapes, one model

A model's cost comes in two shapes. Training is a lump: Meta pretrained Llama 3 405B on 15.6 trillion tokens using 3.8 × 1025 FLOPs of compute on more than 16,000 H100 GPUs; at the 38–43% MFU Meta reports, that is roughly 25 million H100 GPU-hours. Inference is a stream: every token a customer generates costs compute, forever, at a volume that grows with the product. The money has already flipped. Managed inference was $23.1 billion of AI platform infrastructure spend in 2025 against $16.3 billion for training, an inversion from 2022, when training held 74%. Futurum's 2030 base case puts inference at $106.8 billion, 71.7% of the total.

Training behaves like capex, inference like cost of goods sold. Training is lumpy, sunk, and financed. Inference is per-unit, recurring, and tied to revenue. The same model is a capital story on the way in and a volume story on the way out.

The rule of thumb that links them

A standard scaling heuristic for dense models: training a model costs about 6 × parameters × training tokens of compute, and serving it costs about 2 × parameters per generated token. Set them equal and model size cancels: one training run equals the serving compute of 3× its training tokens. For Llama 3 405B, trained on 15.6 trillion tokens, that crossover is about 47 trillion served tokens. Check the arithmetic: 6 × 405 billion × 15.6 trillion ≈ 3.8 × 1025 FLOPs, exactly the training compute Meta reports for the run.

2N serving FLOPs per generated token
3× served vs. trained tokens where the two compute totals are equal

What the meter charges, and where it is heading

Published API prices put the meter in dollars. Anthropic's list prices run from Haiku 4.5 at $1 / $5 per million input / output tokens, through Sonnet 5 at $2 / $10, up to Opus 5 at $5 / $25, with cache reads at 10% of input price and batch jobs at half price. Meanwhile the floor keeps moving: Stanford's AI Index found the cost of querying a GPT-3.5-level model fell from $20 per million tokens in November 2022 to $0.07 by October 2024, a 280-fold drop in 18 months, with per-task declines of 9 to 900× per year. Training runs the other way: Epoch AI estimates frontier training costs have grown about 2.4× per year since 2016, passing $1 billion for the largest runs by 2027, with hardware at 47–67% of model development cost, R&D staff at 29–49%, and energy at 2–6%.

Rules of thumb

1 · Never recover training in the token price.The run is sunk once spent. Price serving off marginal cost and market, and recover training in the volume plan.
2 · Contract along the deflation curve.A multi-year fixed token price signed against today's serving cost looks rich next year. Index, reprice, or keep terms short.
3 · Know your crossover volume.Below roughly 3× training tokens served, the training run is the bigger compute line. Above it, serving dominates. The crossover says whether you are managing a factory or a meter.

  • When we amortize training into unit cost, what served volume does each model need to clear its own economics?

Day 07 · Putting the unit together

Cluster-level contribution margin

Week 1 in one line: price times utilization, minus the costs that run whether or not anyone is billing. A 70% gross margin can still be a loss.

Three margins, three different businesses

GPU clouds look like software above the line and like utilities below it. In fiscal 2025 CoreWeave booked $5.13 billion of revenue against $1.45 billion of reported cost of revenue, a 72% gross margin. Adjusted EBITDA was $3.09 billion, a 60% margin. Then depreciation and amortization took $2.45 billion and net interest took $1.23 billion. GAAP operating income was a $46 million loss, and the net loss was $1.17 billion.

$0.73/hr

Minus $1.14 depreciation ($45,000 ÷ 6 years ÷ 6,570 billable hours) and $0.62 interest ($4,050 a year ÷ 6,570 hours). 24% margin.

The shape matters more than the numbers. About 78% of the cost stack in this example is depreciation and interest, fixed the day the GPU is bought and financed. That is why a 30-point swing in utilization moves the margin from 6% to 37%, and why the backlog matters so much: CoreWeave reported a $66.8 billion revenue backlog at year-end 2025, about 13 times its annual revenue. Contracted hours are what turn the 75% case from an assumption into a forecast.

Rules of thumb

1 · Margin is a utilization function.Quote cluster margin as a curve over utilization, never as a single point. Know the break-even percentage.
2 · EBITDA flatters capital-heavy compute.When depreciation plus interest runs near 70% of revenue, a 60% EBITDA margin can coexist with a net loss.
3 · Contract before you build.With most costs fixed at purchase, the committed contract, not the spot price, sets the real return.

Questions for the CFO table

  • What is each cluster's break-even utilization after depreciation and interest, and how far above it are we running?
  • Which margin do we manage to: contribution, EBITDA, operating, or after financing? Does the board see all four?
  • How much of next year's installed capacity is already covered by committed contracts at known prices?
  • If realized price fell 20% on contract renewal, which clusters would drop below break-even?

Day 06 · Interconnect economics

NVLink, fabric, and stranded compute

A GPU you cannot connect is revenue you cannot book. Interconnect turns chips into clusters; fragmentation turns clusters back into chips.

Three tiers of bandwidth

Sellable capacity is defined by the fabric, not the chip count. Inside a server, NVLink moves data between GPUs at 900 GB/s, seven times PCIe Gen5's 128 GB/s. Across a rack, systems like GB200 NVL72 fuse 72 GPUs into one NVLink domain with 130 TB/s of aggregate bandwidth. Across the data center, InfiniBand or RoCE Ethernet ties thousands of those islands into a training fleet.

Worked example: available is not sellable

Fragmented fleet

600 free, 0 sellable

A cloud has 600 H100s free, scattered across fabric islands of 300 or fewer. A customer wants 512 tightly coupled GPUs for a two-week training run. Illustrative result: the job cannot be placed, and the 600 GPUs are stranded for that workload.

Fused rack

72 as 1

A GB200 NVL72 presents its 72 GPUs as a single NVLink domain that "acts as a single, massive GPU." One placement decision sells the whole rack, and trillion-parameter inference jobs fit inside it.

The goodput tax: when the fabric is slow, GPUs wait on communication instead of computing. Every percentage point of step time spent waiting on all-reduce is a percentage point of GPU capex burned, at fleet scale, forever.

What Meta's two clusters tell us

Meta built two 24k clusters of 24,576 H100 GPUs each for Llama 3 training: one on RoCE Ethernet, one on InfiniBand. At an illustrative $30,000 per GPU, that is roughly $737 million of GPUs per cluster, before switches, optics, and cabling. Running both fabrics side by side was a deliberate hedge: at this scale the interconnect choice is a nine-figure bet, and the largest buyer in the room paid to keep both options alive. Smaller buyers do not get to hedge. They pick one fabric and live with it.

Rules of thumb

1 · Sell domains, not GPUs.The unit of training inventory is the largest contiguous fabric domain, not the fleet total.
2 · Track stranded capacity separately.Idle GPUs that cannot host the jobs in your pipeline are not inventory; they are deferred revenue with a power bill.
3 · Fabric is a structural bet.Ethernet vs InfiniBand vs NVLink domain size locks in which workloads you can serve for the life of the cluster.

Questions for the CFO table

  • What is our largest sellable contiguous domain, and how does it compare with the jobs our customers actually bring?
  • What share of GPU-hours is lost to communication wait, and who measures goodput rather than uptime?
  • Which customer jobs pay a premium for NVLink-domain placement, and do we price that scarcity?
  • What is the switching cost if our fabric bet (Ethernet vs InfiniBand) is wrong for the next model generation?

Day 05 · Memory economics

HBM capacity and bandwidth

On a modern AI accelerator the binding constraint is often memory, not compute. Capacity decides what fits. Bandwidth decides how fast it answers. Both are priced into every GPU-hour.

Two numbers that gate the business

Gigabytes decide which models you can serve; terabytes per second decide how fast. NVIDIA's H100 to H200 upgrade changed almost nothing except memory: same 700W envelope, nearly double the capacity and 1.4x the bandwidth, and roughly 2x LLM inference throughput on Llama2 70B.

80 GB / 3.35 TB/s H100 SXM: HBM3 capacity / bandwidth
141 GB / 4.8 TB/s H200 SXM: HBM3e capacity / bandwidth
~2x NVIDIA's claimed Llama2 70B inference gain, H100 to H200

Worked example: one 70B model

Capacity is a hard gate

2 GPUs or 1

70B parameters at FP16 is about 140 GB of weights: it does not fit on one 80 GB H100, so you buy two GPUs or quantize. At FP8 it is about 70 GB: tight on an H100 once the KV cache lands, comfortable on one 141 GB H200.

Bandwidth is the speed limit

48 vs 68 tok/s

Decoding re-reads the weights for every token. Simplified single-stream roofline: 3.35 TB/s ÷ 70 GB ≈ 48 tokens/s on an H100; 4.8 TB/s ÷ 70 GB ≈ 68 on an H200. Same model, about 1.4x faster from memory alone.

Why the rental premium: an H200 costs more per hour than an H100 not because its math units are faster, but because its memory lets one GPU do what used to take two, and serve each token faster. Memory upgrades convert directly into throughput and capacity revenue.

Why HBM costs what it costs

HBM is stacked DRAM packaged next to the compute die, and it is now the most expensive ingredient in an AI accelerator. One bill-of-materials model puts HBM at about $1,350 of an H100's roughly $3,320 manufacturing cost, and 30-64% of manufacturing cost across flagship parts, with HBM3e contract pricing near $17 per GB. Supply is an oligopoly: Counterpoint's Q2 2025 HBM revenue shares are SK hynix 64%, Micron 21%, and Samsung 15%. And it is largely presold: Micron has said its calendar-2026 HBM supply is fully committed under price-and-volume agreements. When the memory is the margin, the memory market is your market.

Rules of thumb

1 · Capacity before bandwidth.If model plus KV cache does not fit, you are buying a second GPU. Quantization is usually the cheaper lever.
2 · Bandwidth sets decode speed.Tokens per second per stream ≈ bandwidth ÷ model bytes. Batch requests to amortize each weight read.
3 · Watch HBM like a commodity input.It is the largest single cost line in the GPU and is contracted years ahead; price moves flow straight into future hardware cost.

Questions for the CFO table

  • For our target models, how many GPUs does the capacity gate force at FP16 vs FP8, and what does that do to unit cost?
  • What is our achieved tokens/s per GPU against the bandwidth roofline, and how much paid-for headroom sits idle?
  • In multi-year hardware deals, who bears HBM price moves, the buyer or the vendor?
  • Does the next platform upgrade buy us capacity, bandwidth, or both, and which one actually binds our workload?

Day 04 · Capital recovery

Depreciation and useful life

One accounting assumption quietly decides how much capital cost lands in every GPU-hour you sell. Recently, every hyperscaler has moved it, in both directions.

The assumption that moves billions

Depreciation is a non-cash charge, but the useful-life estimate behind it is a pricing decision. Spread a server over more years and each year's reported cost falls; shorten the life and the bill arrives sooner. The hardware, the workload, and the cash are identical. Only the P&L changes.

−$3.9B Alphabet 2023 depreciation after moving servers to 6 years
−$2.92B Meta 2025 depreciation after moving to 5.5 years
+$1.4B Amazon 2025 depreciation after shortening a subset to 5 years

Worked example: one GPU, three accountants

Same GPU, 3-year life

$1.14/hr

$30,000 ÷ (3 × 8,760 hours), straight-line to zero salvage.

Same GPU, 6-year life

$0.57/hr

$30,000 ÷ (6 × 8,760 hours). The metal and the cash did not change.

Utilization bridge: at an illustrative 75% billable utilization, the 6-year assumption prices capital at about $0.76 per billable GPU-hour; the 3-year assumption prices the same GPU at $1.52. A one-line change in an accounting policy note doubles the reported capital cost per sold hour.

Accounting life vs. economic life

The extension wave is real: Microsoft went from 4 to 6 years in 2022, Alphabet from 4 to 6 in 2023, and Meta to 5.5 in 2025. CoreWeave, a pure-play GPU cloud, depreciates its technology equipment straight-line over 6 years. The bulls point to retention evidence: CoreWeave's CEO says its 2020-vintage A100s are fully booked, and 2022 H100s that rolled off a contract were immediately rebooked at about 95% of their original price. Skeptics, including short seller Michael Burry, argue the extensions understate how fast AI chips actually lose value. Amazon is the counter-signal: in 2025 it shortened a subset of servers from 6 years back to 5, citing "the increased pace of technology development, particularly in the area of artificial intelligence and machine learning."

Rules of thumb

1 · Cash left on day one.Depreciation allocates a past outlay across years; it never spends anything. Read capex and free cash flow alongside the P&L charge.
2 · Ask for the life first.A margin or floor price quoted without its depreciation life is not comparable across providers.
3 · Match life to evidence.Contract tenor, rebooking rates, and resale prices are the audit trail for how long a GPU really earns.

Questions for the CFO table

  • What useful life is embedded in our $/GPU-hour floor, and how much does the floor move if the life shortens by one year?
  • What rebooking and resale evidence supports the life we claim, and is it current-generation data or last cycle's?
  • Are customer contract tenors longer or shorter than the depreciation life, and who absorbs the mismatch?
  • If we followed Amazon's reversal, what is the hit to earnings, covenants, and pricing?

Day 03 · Power economics

Power cost per GPU-hour

The GPU's watts are only the first meter. The facility multiplies them, and utilization decides how many billable hours absorb the bill.

Three layers, three denominators

Chip power is not facility power, and facility power is not power cost per billable hour. Start with IT load, apply PUE for facility overhead, then divide by billable utilization to connect electricity spend to revenue-producing hours.

What the simple formula misses

  • Other IT load: CPUs, DRAM, NICs, switches, storage, and fans draw power too.
  • Demand and capacity charges: many contracts charge for peak kW or reserved MW, not only consumed kWh.
  • Load shape: TDP is a ceiling, not measured average workload power.
  • Location and contract: EIA's national industrial average is a reference point, not a data-center tariff.

Rules of thumb

1 · Use PUE on total IT load.Applying PUE to GPU watts alone is a floor. Build the server and network power budget first.
2 · Keep kW and kWh separate.kW is instantaneous capacity; kWh is energy over time. Capacity procurement often binds before energy cost does.
3 · Divide by utilization last.Electricity per installed hour can look small; per billable hour rises as idle capacity increases.

Questions for the CFO table

  • What is measured average kW per GPU by workload, not nameplate TDP?
  • Which charges are energy, demand, transmission, capacity reservation, or pass-through?
  • Is PUE measured at the same boundary across sites, and at what load level?
  • Who bears curtailment risk when power is available but compute cannot run?

Anatomy of $/GPU-hour

The customer sees one price. Finance sees a revenue meter sitting on top of a cost stack.

Start with two different dollars

Quoted $/GPU-hour is a rate. Realized $/installed-GPU-hour is a yield. Multiply the quoted rate by billable utilization before comparing it with costs. Otherwise the numerator is full while the denominator quietly assumes every hour sells.

The cost stack finance should model

  • GPU capital: depreciation or lease expense, financing cost, and obsolescence risk.
  • Power + facility: GPU electricity plus host, cooling, power conversion, rent, and capacity charges.
  • Network + storage: fabric, switches, egress, local storage, and checkpoint traffic.
  • Operations: support, maintenance, spares, orchestration, and credits for downtime.

Rules of thumb

1 · Normalize the denominator.Compare revenue and costs per installed GPU-hour, not a mix of billable and available hours.
2 · Separate floors from allocation.Metered power is observable. Depreciation depends on purchase price, useful life, residual value, and accounting policy.
3 · Bridge rate to yield first.A 25% idle rate turns $3.99 quoted into $2.99 realized before a single cost is deducted.

Questions for the CFO table

  • Which costs scale with billable hours, installed capacity, reserved megawatts, or customers?
  • Does the quoted rate include storage, egress, support, and committed-use discounts?
  • What share of margin variance comes from price, utilization, power, or depreciation?

Day 01 · Capacity economics

Utilization: the bridge from price to profit

A GPU does not earn because it exists. It earns when a customer is running billable work on it.

The concept

Utilization is the percentage of available GPU time that becomes billable GPU time. Headline price per GPU-hour tells you the yield when occupied. Utilization tells you how often you collect it. Together they create realized revenue per installed GPU.

× Utilization billable hours ÷ available hours
= Yield revenue per installed GPU-hour

Worked example: price can lose to occupancy

$1,552/mo

$3,203/mo

Why contracts matter

Reserved-capacity contracts can shift utilization risk from the provider to the customer. In exchange, the customer usually expects a discount, priority access, or both. The provider gives up some spot-price upside but gains revenue visibility and better fixed-cost absorption. The strategic question is not “spot or contract?” It is how much capacity to lock, for how long, and with how much customer concentration.

Rules of thumb

1 · Track yield, not rate.Pair quoted $/GPU-hour with realized utilization. Neither metric is decision-useful alone.
2 · Price cuts need elasticity.A 10% price cut must lift occupied hours by more than 11.1% to grow revenue per installed GPU.
3 · Segment the denominator.Separate available, maintenance, reserved-but-unused, and billable hours. A blended utilization rate can hide the actual constraint.

Questions for the CFO table

  • What is utilization by GPU type, region, customer cohort, and contract form?
  • What share of idle time is demand-driven versus deployment, networking, maintenance, or power?
  • At what utilization does each cluster cover power, hosting, support, and depreciation?
  • How much contracted demand is take-or-pay, and how concentrated is it?
